# After the Signature

A signed decision says a vendor may rotate its access key. That is all it says.

It does not say which rules allowed the rotation, or whether the rotation happened. It says nothing about the engineer who replaced the key by hand in the cloud console during an incident, or about the controller, the program that carries out the change, that lost contact with its authority and acted anyway. Three years later, an auditor holding that decision has to take the operator's word for everything else.

The first articles in this series were about primitives. A sovereign signs what it trusts, recognizes another sovereign with a signed treaty and withdraws that recognition with a signed revocation. Anyone holding its public key can check those decisions without calling it back. That is still the foundation. But a signature answers one question at one moment.

Running a system is everything that happens after it.

The articles that follow are about that. We call it governed change: a change to something that matters is either allowed in advance under rules others can read, or recorded and judged afterward, and either way it leaves signed evidence that can travel to whoever needs to check it. It is portable trust, carried through time. The decisions still belong to the operator, made through the Network Authority, the service each operator runs to hold its keys and sign its trust decisions. What grows is how much of the story after each decision can be shown instead of asserted.

The series takes its questions in order. What exactly was allowed, under which rules, and what was done with it? Can an operator run this for real: in front of other systems, across a boundary with another sovereign, through a failure and an upgrade? What happens when the world does not cooperate, with an outage, an engineer in a hurry, a change that should never have happened or a key that has to be replaced, and how does someone outside check the result years later without taking the operator's word? What may a device do when it is out of reach of its authority? And why should anyone believe the software itself, including the parts we got wrong?

All of it is open source; the project's own demo sovereign runs much of it in public, at mesh.genesismesh.org.

Two things hold throughout. Genesis Mesh does not decide whom an operator should trust, or what its rules should say; the operator does. And nothing here promises that nothing goes wrong, only that what happens leaves a signed record someone else can check.

Trust is not only granted and withdrawn. It is exercised, and every exercise should leave proof.
